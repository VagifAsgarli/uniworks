Artificial Intelligence
Artificial Intelligence (AI) has evolved from an abstract
philosophical concept and mathematical curiosity into one of the
most transformative technological forces of the twenty-first century

•1956. The Dartmouth Conference
Institutional Birth of AI: McCarthy, Minsky, Rochester, and Shannon,
asserted that intelligence could be precisely described and therefore
simulated by machines
Foundational Figures in Early AI
Claude  Herbert Simon
John McCarthy  Marvin Minsky
Shannon  & Allen Newell
• McCarthy: coined “Artificial Intelligence” and developed LISP, central to symbolic AI; focused on formal logic and automated
reasoning (McCarthy, 1988)
• Minsky: intelligence as a collection of interacting computational processes; helped establish cognitive science alongside AI
(Minsky, 1986)
• Shannon: information theory gave a mathematical model of communication, enabling the digital representation of information
(Shannon, 1948)
• Simon & Newell: early cognitive simulation models; human thinking as symbolic problem-solving (Newell and Simon, 1976)

(Simon, 1965), but perception, language ambiguity and

AI Winters, 1970s–1990s
Cause  Effect
Expectations of rapid progress towards human-level AI  Reassessment of general-purpose AI in the short term
• Sustained cuts in funding, confidence and research output after the optimism of the 1950s and 60s.
12
Structural causes of stagnation
Limited compute  Data scarcity
Too little speed, memory and storage; only simplified
No mature internet, so no statistical learning at scale
environments were feasible
13
Symbolic and rule-based systems
Ambiguity  Perception  Common sense  Adaptation
Natural language  Sensory processing  Everyday reasoning  Novel environments
• Good Old-Fashioned AI (GOFAI) relied on explicitly programmed logical rules. Effective in constrained
environments, but brittle in the real world.
14
Overestimated progress
Funding bodies expected rapid breakthroughs. When AI failed to deliver,
funding was cut.
• A cycle of technological hype and disappointment.
15
First AI Winter, 1974–1980
ALPAC report, 1966  Funding cuts, 1970s
Machine translation was far less effective than expected  US and UK agencies reduced investment in AI programmes
16
Second AI Winter, 1987–1993
Expensive  Hard to scale  Manual
Costly to maintain  Narrow and rigid  Dependent on knowledge engineering
• Commercial enthusiasm for expert systems such as MYCIN and XCON collapsed.
• Investment fell sharply, with layoffs and institutional withdrawal.
17
Long-term impact
The winters were not intellectual stagnation but a phase of methodological
reassessment.
• Research moved to probabilistic reasoning, statistics and learning theory, towards data-driven approaches.
18
Rise of machine learning, 1990s–2010
Before  After
Manually encoded rules  Patterns learned directly from data
• A paradigm shift from symbolic AI to statistical, data-driven learning: the foundation of modern AI.

Machine learning, defined
A program learns from experience E with respect to tasks T and performance
measure P, if its performance at T, measured by P, improves with E.
• Mitchell (1997) made machine learning a rigorous scientific discipline.

• Defeated world chess champion Garry Kasparov: specialised AI outperforming human experts in a structured
domain.
• Not general intelligence, but a symbol of the power of narrow AI.

Task 2 · Answer · Algorithm, ML or generative AI?
Fixed rules without learning: plain algorithm. Learned from data: ML or deep
learning. Creates new content: generative AI.
1  Algorithm: fixed arithmetic rules  6 ML: a classifier learned from labelled emails
2  ML: learns from viewing data  7 Algorithm: a fixed timer (an adaptive light would be ML)
3  Deep learning (CNN): learns features from pixels  8 ML: regression on historical prices
4  Algorithm: a fixed sorting rule  9 Generative AI: a diffusion model creates an image
5  Generative AI: an LLM produces new text  10 Rule-based algorithm: nothing is learned
