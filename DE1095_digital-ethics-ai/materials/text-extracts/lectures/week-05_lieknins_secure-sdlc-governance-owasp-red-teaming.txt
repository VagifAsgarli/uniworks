What is a secure development lifecycle (SDLC)?
•  A structured process for building
software with security considered at
every step, not bolted on at the end
•  The alternative - building first,
checking security later - is how most
avoidable incidents happen
•  “Shift left” - the industry phrase for
catching problems as early as possible
in the process
Where security fits - early phases
•  Requirements - what security properties does this system actually need? What's the worst thing that could
happen?
•  Design - threat modeling before a single line of code is written: who might attack this, and how?
•  Build - secure coding practices, and reviewing dependencies you're pulling in
Where security fits - later phases
•  Test - dedicated security testing, not just “does the feature work”
•  Deploy - hardened configuration, least-privilege access, monitoring
switched on from day one
•  Maintain - patching, monitoring, and periodically re-checking your own
assumptions
What's different about a secure AI/LLM development
lifecycle
•  The data itself becomes a security-critical asset, not just the code
•  Threat modeling has to account for the model's behavior, not just the system's code paths - and that behavior
isn't fully predictable
•  You genuinely cannot test every possible input the way you'd test a login form
Threat modeling, traditional app vs AI system
A login form  An AI chatbot
•  Finite set of inputs: username, password, maybe a  •  Input is open-ended natural language - no fixed set
captcha  of “fields”
•  You can enumerate attacks: SQL injection, brute  •  New attack phrasings appear constantly; you can't
force, credential stuffing  enumerate them all
•  Testing looks like: try all the known attack patterns  •  Testing looks like: red-teaming, adversarial probing,
against a fixed set of fields  and accepting you'll never test “everything”

Governance controls: who signs off and when
•  Every stage of the lifecycle
should have a clear owner
and a clear sign-off point
•  Nobody should be able to
push a model update
straight to production
without anyone else
checking it
•  This is where “security”
stops being purely
technical and becomes an
organizational design
question
Four places governance shows up
Data governance  →  Model governance  →  Human oversight  →  Documentation & traceability
Data governance controls
•  Who's allowed to add data to the training set?
•  Is there a review process before new data actually gets used?
•  This directly addresses the data-poisoning risk from week 4 - poisoning is much harder when data can't just be
added unreviewed
What happens without data governance
•  A team scrapes a large public dataset quickly to hit a deadline, with no review of what's actually in it
•  Months later, the model shows a strange bias - it turns out a chunk of the scraped data came from a forum full
of one very specific, skewed viewpoint
•  Nobody can say who approved using that source, because nobody had to approve it
Model governance controls
•  Testing and validation required before a model can be deployed
•  Clear, written criteria for what counts as “good enough” to go live - not a gut call
•  Someone specific has to actually approve the release
Model governance doing its job
•  A company requires a red-team review before any customer-facing model update ships
•  A new version passes normal functional tests, but the red team finds it's newly vulnerable to a jailbreak pattern
from three weeks ago
•  Release is held, the fix goes in, and it ships a week later instead of that day
Human oversight requirements
•  The EU AI Act requires
human oversight for high-risk
systems
•  In practice: humans who can
understand what the system
is doing, and who can
override or shut it down
•  “A human somewhere in the
loop” isn't enough if that
human can't meaningfully act
Worked example: real oversight vs a rubber stamp
Rubber-stamp “oversight”  Meaningful oversight
•  A human clicks “approve” on hundreds of AI loan  •  The system flags low-confidence or unusual cases
decisions a day  for a human to actually review
•  No time or information to actually evaluate any single  •  That human has the model's reasoning, relevant
one  history, and the authority to reverse the decision
•  Exists on paper, does nothing in practice  •  Fewer decisions reviewed, but each one is a real
check
Documentation & traceability
•  Why “who decided this” matters - for audits, for accountability, and for learning from mistakes
•  If something goes wrong, can you actually reconstruct what happened and why?
•  The AI Act explicitly requires this for high-risk systems: technical documentation and logging
The cost of missing documentation
•  A regulator asks a company to explain why a specific high-risk model version was approved for deployment
•  The engineer who approved it left the company eight months ago. There's no written record of the reasoning,
only a Slack message that's since been deleted
•  The company can't answer the question - which is itself a compliance failure, independent of whether the
original decision was even reasonable
Bringing the four controls together
•  Picture a real launch checklist for a new AI feature at a mid-size company
•  Data: “has the training data been reviewed and signed off by the data team?”
•  Model: “has this version passed validation and red-team review?”
•  Human oversight: “is there a named person who can override this in production?”
•  Documentation: “is there a written record of who approved this, and why?”
•  Four yes/no questions - unglamorous, but this is genuinely what separates a mature process from a reckless
one
Governance failure, step by step
•  A company's customer-service model gets a routine-seeming update
•  It's a small tweak - retrained on three months of newer chat logs, meant to keep it “current”
•  Nobody flags it as needing the full review process, because “it's just a refresh”
What went wrong
•  Those three months of chat logs included a period where a coordinated group was deliberately feeding the bot
rude, aggressive phrasing to “train” it - a low-effort version of a poisoning attempt
•  The refresh baked in a chunk of that tone; the model becomes noticeably ruder in ambiguous customer
complaints
•  Nobody notices for weeks, because nobody was specifically watching for a tone shift after a “minor” update
What should have caught it
•  Data governance: any
retraining, even a “refresh”
should trigger a review of
what's actually in the new
data
•  Model governance: any
update should require
validation against known-
good behavior before
shipping, not just “it still
answers questions”
•  The lesson: the size of a
change is not a good
proxy for whether it needs
review
Kahoot quiz: governance control or technical control?
Quiz summary
•  Governance: two-person approval, the review committee, written justification - these are process and people
decisions
•  Technical: profanity filtering, rate-limiting - these are things the system does automatically, no human decision
in the moment
•  Most real systems need both - one without the other leaves a gap

What is OWASP?
What is OWASP, briefly
•  Open Worldwide Application Security Project
•  A well-known nonprofit that publishes widely-used, free security guidance
•  Famous for the “OWASP Top 10” - the most critical risks in a given category, kept updated by the security
community
•  Not a law or a standard - a practical, “battle-tested” checklist
Why a checklist matters
•  You designed governance controls from scratch for one system
•  Most teams don't start from a blank page - they start from a published checklist and adapt it
•  The OWASP Top 10 for LLMs is exactly that kind of starting point, specifically for AI systems
Introducing the OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications
•  A specific version of that
list, focused entirely on
LLM-powered
applications
•  Ten categories of risk,
numbered LLM01
through LLM10
LLM01:2025 - Prompt Injection
•  Covered this in-depth last week - direct and indirect
•  Applied to our HR bot: an employee crafts a message that gets the bot to ignore its “only answer HR
questions” instruction
•  Still item one on the list, in both the old and new editions - it's consistently the most common LLM-specific risk
in practice
LLM02:2025 - Sensitive Information Disclosure
•  The model reveals information it shouldn't - through training data, or through what it's given access to at
runtime
•  Applied to our HR bot: it has access to employee records, and a cleverly-phrased question gets it to reveal one
employee's salary to another
•  Connects directly to membership inference from last week and to GDPR
LLM03:2025 - Supply Chain
•  Vulnerabilities anywhere in the LLM pipeline: pre-trained models, plugins, datasets, fine-tuning services, or
libraries you didn't build yourself
•  Applied to our HR bot: it's built on a third-party foundation model, and a plugin it uses for scheduling gets
compromised
•  You're only ever as secure as the weakest link you didn't build
LLM04:2025 - Data and Model Poisoning
•  Corrupting what the model learns - during pre-training, fine-tuning, or even embedding generation
•  Applied to our HR bot: if it was fine-tuned on past support tickets, and some of those tickets contained
manipulation attempts, that behavior can get baked in
•  Broader than last week's “data poisoning” - this item now explicitly covers the model itself, not just the training
data feeding it
LLM05:2025 - Improper Output Handling
•  Insufficient validation, sanitization, or filtering of whatever the model generates, before it's used or displayed
•  Applied to our HR bot: it generates a database command based on a request, and that command gets
executed directly, with no validation
•  The fix: treat model output the same way you'd treat any untrusted user input - validate before acting
LLM06:2025 - Excessive Agency
•  Giving a model more autonomy or permissions than the task actually requires - this now also absorbs what
used to be a separate “insecure plugin design” item
•  Applied to our HR bot: it was given full read-and-write access to employee records, when it only ever needed to
update leave balances
•  This is the AI-flavored version of least-privilege - the access-control principle from earlier in the lifecycle
discussion
LLM07:2025 - System Prompt Leakage
•  The hidden instructions that shape how a model behaves - its “system prompt” - can sometimes be extracted
by a user just by asking cleverly
•  Applied to our HR bot: a curious employee gets it to reveal the internal instructions telling it what it's allowed
and not allowed to discuss
•  This is genuinely new to the 2025 list, and it matters because a leaked system prompt often hands an attacker
the exact wording to target with prompt injection
LLM08:2025 - Vector and Embedding Weaknesses
•  Security gaps in systems that use vector databases and embeddings - the technology behind giving a model
access to a company's own documents
•  Applied to our HR bot: if it looks up answers from a shared vector database of HR documents, one employee's
confidential document could leak into another employee's search results if access isn't properly separated
•  Also new to the 2025 list - reflects how common this “retrieval” pattern has become since the earlier list was
written
LLM09:2025 - Misinformation
•  The model generates convincing, plausible-sounding false information, and a user accepts and acts on it
without verifying
•  Applied to our HR bot: it confidently states an incorrect leave-policy detail, and an employee acts on it without
checking
•  Related to last week's overreliance idea, but the emphasis shifted: this item is about the false content itself, not
just the human's habit of trusting it too much
LLM10:2025 - Unbounded Consumption
•  Unrestricted use of model resources - whether from an attack, or just uncontrolled legitimate growth - leading
to runaway costs or degraded service
•  Applied to our HR bot: someone sends a flood of extremely long, complex requests, driving up cost and
slowing the system for everyone else
•  This absorbs what used to be a narrower “model denial of service” item - and notice, “model theft” as its own
separate item is gone from the current list entirely
The OWASP Top 10 for LLMs (2025)
1. Prompt Injection
2. Sensitive Information Disclosure
3. Supply Chain
4. Data and Model Poisoning
5. Improper Output Handling
6. Excessive Agency
7. System Prompt Leakage
8. Vector and Embedding Weaknesses
9. Misinformation
10. Unbounded Consumption
Using the Top 10 as a practical checklist
•  Not just theory - teams actually sit down and walk through this list before launching a system
•  For each item: does this apply to us? If yes, what's our specific mitigation?
•  It won't catch everything - no checklist does - but it catches the common, well-understood failures cheaply

What is red teaming?
•  A dedicated team deliberately tries to break your system before real attackers do
•  Adopts an attacker's mindset on purpose, rather than checking “does this work as intended”
•  You've actually been doing a version of this in every breakout task this course - now it gets a name
Red teaming vs regular QA testing
QA testing  Red teaming
•  Asks: “does this work the way it's supposed to?”  •  Asks: “how can I make this do something it's not
supposed to?”
•  Tests expected use cases and known edge cases
•  Deliberately tries unexpected, adversarial, and
•  Success = the system behaves correctly
creative inputs
•  Success = you found a real weakness before an
attacker did
Red teaming and the AI Act
•  Remember from Roberts' week 2: systemic-risk GPAI models are legally required to undergo adversarial
testing
•  Red teaming is literally how that legal requirement gets satisfied in practice
•  This is a case where “good practice” and “legal requirement” are the exact same activity
Who actually does this?
•  In-house red teams - employees whose job is specifically to attack the company's own systems
•  External red-teamers - specialist firms hired specifically for this, bringing an outside perspective
•  Bug bounty programs - the public is invited to find and report flaws, usually for a reward
•  Larger, higher-stakes systems tend to use more than one of these at once
What makes a good red team
•  Technical skill matters, but isn't the whole story
•  Diversity of background and thinking - people who'd try angles the original builders never considered, because
they don't share the builders' assumptions
•  Genuine independence from the team that built the system - hard to red-team your own work objectively
The limits of red teaming
•  It finds specific weaknesses - it doesn't prove a system is safe, only that these particular attempts didn't work
•  You still cannot test every possible input; red teaming samples the space, it doesn't cover it
•  This is exactly why it works alongside governance and the OWASP checklist, not instead of them
Why “secure by design” beats “secure by patching”
•  Patching after the fact means you're always playing catch-up - the attacker moves first, every time
•  Building security in from the start - governance, checklists, red teaming - means fewer surprises, and faster
recovery when surprises still happen
•  Neither approach makes a system perfectly safe - but one of them is a lot less exhausting to run
Back to ethics: rules and virtue, working together
•  The governance controls we covered today - sign-offs, documentation, required reviews - are essentially
Kantian: fixed rules, followed without exception, regardless of how routine a change feels
•  Red teaming and human oversight require something more Aristotelian: judgment, adapting to a specific, novel
situation you couldn't fully write a rule for in advance
•  A mature security culture needs both - firm rules for the routine, and trained judgment for what the rules didn't
anticipate
